# Information architecture

Model the domain people must recognize, understand, and find, not the implementation that stores it. Inventory concepts, content, actions, relationships, terminology, audiences, permissions, lifecycle, scale, and evidence of the words people actually use. Treat internal team ownership, database tables, or current navigation as inputs to inspect, not default organizing principles.

Use one hierarchy only where a primary parent-child relationship is truthful. Introduce facets, cross-links, search/filters, contextual entry points, or multiple routes when a single tree would distort the domain or force unlike things together. Keep categories mutually intelligible even when they cannot be perfectly exclusive.

Labels should be specific, distinguishable, understandable without hidden context, and grounded in task/user language. Preserve orientation: people should be able to tell where they are, recognize destinations, recover from a wrong route, and reach important content through appropriate alternate paths. Do not rely on fixed navigation-item or taxonomy-size counts as universal rules.

Verify findability with representative tasks and realistic wording. Record where people start, what label/path they expect, choices/backtracking, successful destination, and failure patterns. Tree tests or equivalent evidence can isolate hierarchy/label strength; stakeholder preference alone is not findability evidence.

Keep behavior/state transitions with `interaction-design`, visual treatment with `visual-interface-design`, and storage normalization with engineering.
